Fidelity drift, and the reset

None of this is a competence problem. It is a repetition problem — the techniques you know, worn smooth by four hundred sessions. Find the one that is you right now.

How the device is built

Core words — want, stop, more, go, help, that, done — are a small set used constantly across every activity, and they carry roughly 80% of everything anyone says all day. Fringe words — dinosaur, Takis, grandma — are specific, motivating, and endless, but they are the last mile, not the highway. Model core first: a core word transfers to every situation, a fringe word fits only one.

You never rearrange the buttons. A fluent user reaches a word the way you reach a key on a keyboard — without looking, because the spot lives in the hand. Move it and you erase that memory; the word is still on screen and now lost. "Let me just customize it for him" is the most dangerous sentence in AAC. Changes go through the SLP, never mid-session. Hide or mask cells if a page is too much — never move or delete the survivors.

Modeling, not quizzing

The quiz is seductive. It gives you a response, and a response feels like progress. But a prompted correct is not a spontaneous correct. If your session is all quizzing, your data is measuring your prompt, not the student's language.

Three resets: one or two key words per utterance, not every word. Model to show, not to test. And retire "use your words" — model, then pause. Aided language input is effective across ages, disabilities, and language levels; the question at this point in your career is the quality of the input, not whether you do it.

Wait time and the prompt hierarchy

Ten to fifteen seconds of expectant silence. Most of us give three, and three seconds is not a chance to respond — it is a chance to watch us answer for them. Count it silently and let it feel too long, because too long for you is about right for the student.

Cues creep. A light cue fails once, you go heavier, and heavier quietly becomes the new baseline. Weeks later you are doing hand-over-hand and never decided to. Start every prompt at the lightest level that has worked before, not the heaviest that guarantees a response.

1Fix the environment. Device in reach, always
2Pause. Ten to fifteen seconds, expectant face
3Model it yourself
4Gesture toward the area, not the exact button
5Partial verbal: "you can tell me." Then stop

No hand-over-hand on communication devices — it makes the message yours, not theirs, and a student who cannot refuse the touch cannot consent to it. Hand-under-hand written into a plan is the one exception. Support the arm, never the message.

Gestalt language processing

Most of us are analytic processors: single words first, then phrases, building language up from parts. A gestalt processor runs the other way — whole chunks first (a movie line, a phrase used as one unit), broken down into flexible pieces later. Many autistic students are gestalt processors, and echolalia is not a problem to stop; it is a meaningful stage.

What it changes: don't drill single words with a gestalt processor. Model rich, natural chunks they can reuse. Honor the echo — respond to what they mean, not the words in isolation. A gestalt on the device is language in progress. You don't run GLP therapy (that's the SLP's plan), but you should recognize it and stop the one thing that hurts: single-word drill.

Meet the student where they are

The same input helps one student and holds another back — the difference is where they are on the arc. Emergent: no reliable symbolic communication yet; model constantly, expect nothing back, build the map. Context-dependent: communicates in familiar situations only; widen it — same words, new settings, new people. Independent: generates novel messages across partners; step back, expand and recast. Modeling never stops; what changes is how much you expect back.

Presume competence — the least dangerous assumption. Talk at age level. Narrate what you do. Never talk over a student's head. Assume understanding outruns output: what they show you is the floor of what they know, never the ceiling.

When the session falls apart

The ten minutes a week nobody trained you for. The lens: behavior is communication. Every function is a message — a student who bolts is saying "I need out," a student who sweeps materials is saying "too hard." Treat the message as data, not as the session being ruined.

Three moves when it climbs. Stop talking, three words or fewer — during escalation they cannot take words in. Model, do not demand. Honor the message, every time and in any form. If the student communicates "break," the break happens. That is the session working, not failing.

The escape reconciliation: honoring the break request does not add escape — the student already escapes by screaming. You are changing the route. Fade the duration later, never the reliability. And the SLPA boundary: you follow a behavior plan, you do not write or change it. Run the communication piece with fidelity, and flag what you see.

Data your SLP can use

Two habits. Measure the student, not the prompt — prompted and independent are different columns, and blurring them makes the whole treatment plan drift. Know what to flag up — exit criteria met, a goal that no longer fits, weeks of non-communicative use, a plan that isn't working. You don't fix these; you flag them, clearly, with the data to back it. You are the eyes in the room your supervising SLP is not always in.

And read the data, not just take it. A wall of prompted responses means the target is too hard or the prompt is stuck, not "he needs more prompting." A flat line for weeks means the plan needs a change, not more pushing. Independent creeping up means it's working — plan the next fade. Great in one setting only means the goal is now generalization. You don't change the plan; you read it well enough to tell your SLP what it's saying.
