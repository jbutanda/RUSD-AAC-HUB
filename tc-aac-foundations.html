What AAC really is

AAC is everything but speech. The device carries the most, but signs, gestures, and pointing all count, and none of it waits for the device to be perfect. Our shared job is simple to say and takes practice to do: model language on his system, honor every attempt, and keep his voice ready and in reach — at home and at school.

What he uses: TouchChat with WordPower

WordPower is a word-based vocabulary — built to make sentences on the fly, not just pick single pictures. It's designed around core words by speech-language pathologist Nancy Inman, and it's current in the 2025 version of TouchChat. It grows with him, from a handful of words up to a full keyboard.

A generic illustration of a word-based grid — core words in fixed spots, category pages for fringe. His actual layout depends on his WordPower level.

Core words, and why the layout never moves

A few hundred core words do roughly 80% of all talking — small, reusable, everywhere. Fringe words (dinosaur, pizza, Minecraft) are specific and motivating, but each fits only one moment. We model core first, because a core word works in every situation.

The buttons never move. A fluent user reaches a word the way you reach a key on a keyboard — without looking, because the spot lives in the hand. Moving a button erases that memory. So changes go through the SLP, we hide rather than delete, and we keep the same word in the same spot every day, home and school.

Modeling is the one skill

A child hears a language for about a year before their first word. Modeling gives the device the same fair start: you talk while touching the words. Touch one or two key words, model to show rather than quiz, and retire "use your words" — model instead, then wait a silent ten. For the behavior team: this is aided language input, and it's errorless — no demand, no wrong answer, no extinction burst.

Do this

Touch the words as you say them. Model, then wait. Honor every attempt — a tap, a look, a gesture, a word.

Skip this

No quizzing, no "use your words," and no moving buttons. Exploration isn't misuse — it's babbling, and it's how language grows.

Behavior is communication

Every function is a message: escape means "I need out," attention means "talk to me," tangible means "I want that." Teach the word and the behavior loses its job — that's functional communication training on his device. It maps directly onto what the behavior team already does: errorless teaching, prompt fading, replacement behaviors, and data.

The escape question, answered: honoring the break request doesn't add escape — he already escapes by melting down. You're changing the route to a communicative one. Fade duration later; never fade the reliability of the request.

A low-tech backup for the hardest moments: when the device is out of reach or he's too dysregulated to navigate it, a printed de-escalation board keeps a channel open. It's a bridge, not a replacement for his device. Printable boards for each grade band are in the downloads below.

AAC for academics

Requesting is the floor, not the ceiling. A device that only asks for snacks and breaks is a remote control, not a voice. Academic access means he can comment, answer, question, compare, and object — about math, a story, a science lesson. The research calls this Project Core: core vocabulary taught inside real academic routines.

One flexible core word does academic work in every subject — he doesn't need a new word for every lesson.

Four moves that work in any lesson: model during instruction (touch core words as you teach), word of the week (saturate every subject with one core word), describe instead of label-hunt ("it's big and fast" generalizes; hunting for the exact noun doesn't), and ask real questions, then wait — give him room to have an idea. The long game is literacy: WordPower's keyboard opens every word in the language as his spelling grows.

Which words? A small set of flexible core words carries most academic talk — words for comparing (more, same, different), describing (big, fast, hard), cause and change (make, stop, change), and questioning (what, why, not). One word travels across every subject: "more than" in math, "it changed" in science, "what happens" in reading. The daily habit: the VOICE check

Visible. On. In reach. Charged. Every setting. Five seconds before each activity, home and school. A device in the backpack is a voice that can't ask for anything.
