Being a good partner to a strong AAC user

He's mastering the device fast, so our job is to keep up and grow with him. The core skill is modeling, talking while touching the words, and for a student this far along, model a little above where he is: longer sentences, new words. Show, don't quiz. Ask real questions and wait. You don't have to be fluent yourself to be a great partner.

Visible. On. In reach. Charged. Every setting. Five seconds, everywhere, and for a high schooler that now includes work-based learning and the community. A device in a backpack can't talk.

Give him the words a teenager actually uses

AAC is self-expression, so his device should sound like a sixteen-year-old, not a young child. Draw vocabulary from his real life:

friends' namesmusic & gameshis classesslanginside jokesopinions"leave me alone"relationships

That includes relationship language and the strong words his peers use. A speaking teenager can gossip, flirt, and swear, and gatekeeping that language from an AAC user isn't protection, it's denying him the ability to sound like himself. He gets the real words.

Growing the system

A fast learner is a reason to expand, not to sit still. When he masters what's in front of him, reveal more words and move to a bigger grid, using Progressive Language so the buttons he already knows never move. More language, same motor plan.

Grow the grid as he grows, revealing words in fixed positions rather than starting small.

Literacy, the ceiling-lifter

Spelling unlocks every word in the language, anything he wants to say, any time, with no one needed to add it first. Keep the keyboard in reach, pair symbols with text, and remember that literacy is power that cannot be taken away. He'll return the school device someday; the reading and writing he builds are his forever.

Self-advocacy and his lead

Adolescent AAC is about autonomy. Teach him to tell partners how to support him ("please wait while I find my word"), to repair being misunderstood, to make real choices including refusals and privacy, and to help lead his own IEP. At 18, his educational decisions become legally his, build toward that now.

Getting him his own device, and why it matters now

The school device goes back. It belongs to the district, and when he graduates or ages out (up to 22), he returns it. For a young man who communicates this well, that can't mean losing his voice, so the time to secure a personal device is while he's still in school and the team can help.

Who pays decides who owns it. If the school buys the device, it belongs to the school. If insurance or another funder pays, it belongs to him, and goes everywhere he goes, for life. That's the whole reason to pursue a personal device.

The funding ladder

A speech device is durable medical equipment. Work down the ladder in order. Every route rests on an SLP evaluation and a doctor's order of medical necessity.

  1. Private insurance. Most plans cover speech-generating devices as durable medical equipment with documented medical need. If you have private insurance, start here.
  2. Medi-Cal. Covers speech devices with prior authorization. A dedicated, locked device is approved more readily than a consumer tablet, because Medi-Cal funds equipment made for disability. The SLP or device vendor usually completes the complex paperwork.
  3. Regional Center. A payer of last resort after insurance and Medi-Cal; can also fund assistive technology and related supports.
  4. California Voice Options (Dept. of Rehabilitation). The state's final backstop, provides a device (an iPad with a speech app, including Proloquo2Go) so no Californian goes without a voice. Requires exhausting insurance first and an SLP evaluation.

The easiest place to start: the device makers' funding teams. Tobii Dynavox, PRC-Saltillo, and Lingraphica each assemble and submit your insurance/Medi-Cal paperwork and lend a device to trial for about four weeks. For a family new to this, one phone call to a funding team is the simplest first step.

The IEP, transition, and the handoff

The IEP is the legal bridge from the school device to his own. Two documents do the work:

The transition plan, required in the IEP by age 16, sets goals for after high school. His communication system and the recommendation for a personal device belong in it, in writing. The Summary of Performance, which the district must provide when he exits, summarizes his skills, needs, accommodations, and evaluations, and carries his AAC into college, work, and adult life. Ask for updated AAC and educational evaluations before he graduates.
