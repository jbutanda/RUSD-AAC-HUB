Start Monday — the whole ask

  1. One student. Pick one to start with.
  2. One core word. Put it on a sticky note where you'll see it.
  3. Model it all week, on the device, in every subject — a few times an hour.
  4. Ask a real question, then wait a silent ten.
  5. Invite the SLP in to model with you. That's the request we most want.

The core idea

He doesn't need the lesson's vocabulary to think about the lesson. "Photosynthesis" and "denominator" are fringe words — he can label the topic, but labeling isn't thinking. The words that let him reason — it changed, it needs light, more, different, why — are core words he already has. The specific noun names the topic; core words let him have an idea about it.

Why it's worth the effort: reviews put AAC device abandonment at 30–50%, and the reason isn't the hardware — it's follow-through. Think of AAC as 20% device, 80% what happens in your room every day. That 80% is the part only you can do.

Four myths that block academic access

Most of us were taught these as fact. The evidence has moved.

  • "He has to master the basics before he's ready for academics." No cognitive or language prerequisites exist for AAC. Waiting for a student to "prove readiness" withholds the tool he needs to show it.
  • "He can't learn grade-level content." Debunked in the research, yet it persists. With evidence-based instruction, all learners grow.
  • "He can't do academics until he can read." Literacy isn't a gate. Teach content with core words now; alphabet access grows alongside, not after. (Erickson & Koppenhaver)
  • "He needs to show cause-and-effect first." The device teaches cause and effect — every press that produces a word is cause and effect in action.

The least dangerous assumption: when we're unsure what a student can do, presuming he can and teaching him is far safer than doing nothing — because doing nothing has a guaranteed cost.

How to model during instruction

Aided language input means you talk while touching the words — it's not a separate speech session, it's a way of teaching the lesson you already teach. Three rules: touch one or two key words as you say them, model to show rather than quiz, and ask a real question then wait a silent ten.

Same words, three subjects. Read-aloud: "The character CHANGED — he feels DIFFERENT. What do you THINK happens?" Math: "We need MORE. Is it the SAME or DIFFERENT?" Science: "It STOPPED. Something HAPPENED. Why?" Six core words, all thinking.

What it looks like in each subject

The same handful of core words drives access across the curriculum — no new vocabulary per subject.

Reading & ELA

Your move: as you read aloud, model the thinking words — touch what the character feels, ask what will happen, let him predict and react. He can say: "what happens next" (predicting), "he feels different" (describing a character), "I like it / not that" (reacting), "that's not right" (objecting).

Math

Your move: narrate quantity and comparison — is it more or less, the same or different? Let him compare and describe, not just name numbers. He can say: "more / less" (comparing), "same or different" (sorting), "put it here" (sequence), "not the same" (noticing inequality).

Science

Your move: observation is describing and questioning — pure core. Model as you demonstrate: it changed, it stopped, what happened, why? He can say: "it changed" (observing), "more heat" (cause & effect), "what happened" (questioning), "why" (reasoning).

Social Studies

Your move: compare then and now, near and far, and invite opinion. He can say: "same / different" (then & now), "I think" (opinion), "good / bad" (evaluating), "who / where" (people and places).

Notice it's the same core words every time — more, different, change, what, why, think. He doesn't need a biology word to think in science or a math word to reason in math. He needs core, modeled while you teach. Use the lesson planner to map a real lesson of your own.

It's everyone's to build

The device belongs to the student, and it works best when everyone feeds it. You know your classroom's language better than anyone, so add the lesson and topic words your teaching needs — don't wait on the SLP. Home adds family and daily-life words; the SLP builds the core backbone and coaches. This is the model we're advocating for (there's no district policy yet).

The one rule that makes it work: the core stays put. Add fringe and topic vocabulary freely in your lane, but never move a core button — that's the muscle memory the student navigates by. Back up your changes and loop in the SLP, who keeps the layout safe and catches drift. Not a gate — support.

And support is ongoing: teach, model, coach, review. Ask the SLP to come model in your room — that's the request we most want to get.

The VOICE check

Visible. On. In reach. Charged. Every setting. Five seconds before each lesson. None of the academic access above is possible if the device is in the backpack. Assign someone to own the daily check.
