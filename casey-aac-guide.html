Where we start

Communication is a basic human right. AAC gives our students a way to request, refuse, comment, ask questions, share opinions, joke around, and speak up for themselves. Wants and needs are only the starting point. Every adult in a child’s life is part of that voice.

What is AAC?

Augmentative and Alternative Communication is any way of communicating that adds to speech or takes the place of speech. Most communicators use a mix of all three kinds below, and that is expected and healthy.

1

No tech

Gestures, signs, facial expression, body language, pointing, leading you by the hand.

2

Low tech

Picture boards, communication books, printed core boards, choice cards, writing.

3

High tech

Speech-generating devices and apps like Proloquo2Go and TD Snap on an iPad.

Who uses AAC at Casey?

  • Students with little or no speech, including many students in our ESN and MMSN classrooms. AAC is their primary voice right now.
  • Students whose speech is hard to understand. They talk, but not clearly enough for everyone, every time. AAC fills the gaps and reduces breakdowns.
  • Students who speak but struggle under stress. Speech can shut down when a child is upset, tired, or overwhelmed. A device or board is a backup voice in the hard moments.
No one has to earn it first. There are no prerequisite skills for AAC. No test to pass, no pictures to match first, no cause-and-effect to prove. Access to AAC is how readiness is built.

Myths and facts

These are the questions families and staff ask most often. Most of us were taught the myths as fact. The evidence has moved.

“If she uses a device, she will stop trying to talk.”
AAC does not reduce speech. A review of the research (Millar, Light & Schlosser, 2006) found no case where speech decreased and most cases where speech increased. For many children, AAC supports more spoken words.
“AAC is a last resort, after speech therapy hasn’t worked.”
AAC and speech work together from the start. AAC is language input, not a sign that anyone has given up on speech, and earlier access predicts better outcomes for both.
“She is too young, or too involved, to use this.”
Children learn language from birth by seeing it used. AAC works the same way, at any age or level. The students with the most significant needs are exactly who benefits most from early access.
“The device is for asking for things.”
AAC is for all communication: comments, questions, jokes, opinions, and saying no. A device that only requests is a remote control, not a voice.

Core words and fringe words

Core words are a small set of words that makes up about 80% of what we say every day. They work in every room, every subject, every routine, which is why we model them constantly. Fringe words are the specific nouns tied to one place, person, or topic: pencil, swing, pizza, Grandma. Children need both.

Best practices

Presume competence

“You don’t have to be a technology expert to communicate with children who use these devices. Just be willing to learn, engage the student directly, and welcome all efforts to communicate.”  ASHA President, 2026
  • Talk at age level. A seven-year-old who uses a device is seven. Use the same words, jokes, and tone you would with any classmate.
  • Assume the idea is there. Our job is not to decide whether a child has a thought. It is to give her the words for the one she already has.
  • Understanding outruns output. What a child can show us is the floor of what he knows, never the ceiling.

Modeling: use the device when you talk

This is called modeling, or aided language input. It is how every child learns language: by seeing and hearing it used for real reasons, thousands of times, long before they say a first word. A child who uses AAC needs to see us use their system the same way. It is the strongest evidence-based strategy we have.

1

Talk and tap

Say the whole sentence out loud. Tap one or two key words on the device as you say them. Not every word.

2

One step ahead

Model one or two words beyond what the child uses now. One word now? Model two-word combinations.

3

No pressure

Never require the child to copy you. Modeling is showing, not testing. No “show me,” no “what’s this?”

W

Wait

Pause at least 10 seconds. Count in your head. Silence is thinking time and motor planning time. Most adults wait about 3 seconds; our students need 10 to 15.

N

Notice

Watch for every attempt: a tap, a look, a reach, a sign, a sound, a lean toward the board.

R

Respond

Treat every message as meaningful, even if it seems random. Say it back and build on it. If the child says “stop,” honor it when it is safe.

Rethinking the prompt hierarchy

Most of us were trained on a prompt ladder that climbs from a verbal cue to a gesture to a physical prompt until the child produces the target. The newer thinking flips the goal: lower the pressure and invite, rather than escalate until the child complies. Rachael Langley’s reframe is the clearest version of this, and it is what we ask every adult at Casey to use.

Healthy habits

  • Thoughtful pause. Be mindful of how much you talk. Pause and wait without pressure. A pause is an invitation to join in.
  • Show interest with your face and body. Let the child see you are listening and curious about what they are thinking.
  • Observe and comment. “I wonder if you are ready to GO,” while you tap GO. An “I wonder” or “I think” comment, not a question or a demand.
  • Model without expectation. “I LIKE your shoes!” Tap LIKE. Make comments that do not require the child to answer.

Stop

  • Modeling so they copy you. “I said I want cookie, now you say it.” It looks helpful, but children need to know they choose their own words.
  • Prompting to make them say it. “Touch circle. I’ll help you touch circle. Tell me circle.” Nobody should be made to say words, and hand-over-hand is more harmful than helpful.

Adapted from Revisiting the AAC Prompt Hierarchy, Rachael Langley, MA, CCC-SLP, 2023.

No physical prompts

We do not move a child’s hand to the words. Hand-over-hand looks like help. What it teaches is that someone else decides what the child says, when, and with which hand. The message stops being theirs, the child learns to wait for the adult, and many children start pulling away from the device altogether.

What to do instead:

1

Model it yourself

Tap the word on their device, or on your own copy or a paper board, as you say it. They see the path without being moved through it.

2

Point or gesture

Point near the word, tap the edge of the device, or hold it closer. Gestures invite; hands on hands direct.

3

Wait longer than feels right

Ten to fifteen seconds. Most of the time the child was already on the way.

4

Accept what comes

A look, a reach, a sign, or a nearby tap all count. Say it back and build on it.

If a student has a motor need that calls for physical support to access the device, that is a plan the SLP and OT make together and write down, not something to improvise in the moment. A light touch at the elbow as a cue is different from guiding the hand to a button. When in doubt, keep your hands on your own device.

What helps and what gets in the way

Do

  • Talk to the child at age level
  • Keep the device within reach all day
  • Accept speech, signs, gestures, boards, and the device
  • Create real reasons to communicate
  • Model without expecting a response
  • Tell the SLP or teacher about missing words

Avoid

  • Taking the device away as a consequence
  • Quizzing (“Show me ___,” “What’s this?”)
  • Speaking for the child when they can respond
  • Moving or deleting buttons
  • Saving the device for “speech time” only
  • Hand-over-hand pressing for them. A light touch at the elbow or pointing to the word is enough.

Behavior is communication

When a child does not have the words, the body does the talking. Our job is to read the message, then give the child the words for it. Every one of the four messages below is also a communication function, and the words for all four are core words already on the device.

The messageWhat we might seeWords we can model
Escape or avoidPushing work away, dropping to the floor, running“I don’t want this.” “I need a break.” “Too hard.” “All done.”
Get somethingGrabbing, crying at the snack cabinet, pulling your hand“I want that.” “More.” “My turn.”
Connection or attentionPoking a peer, throwing to get a reaction, climbing on you“Look at me.” “Play with me.” “Hi!”
Sensory or comfortCovering ears, rocking, chewing, seeking pressure“Too loud.” “I feel bad.” “I need quiet.”

A child who can say no does not have to hit to say no. Protest words (stop, no, not, don’t like, break) are some of the most important words on the device. Model them at the moment the behavior is happening, and honor them when it is safe.

When the device is pushed away

A device on the floor is not a refusal of communication. It is usually a message about the moment. Here is the script the whole team uses the same way.

Stop talkingFewer words, lower voice, slower pace. A dysregulated brain cannot process language.
Model, don’t demandPick up the device or board yourself and model: “stop,” “break,” “all done.” No “use your words.”
Honor the messageIf the message is “no” or “break,” give it when it is safe. That is what teaches the words work.
Bring it back calmlyOnce the child is settled, the device returns within reach with zero comment. Then go on with the day.
A printed core board is the backup for these moments. It cannot break, it cannot run out of battery, and it keeps a channel open when the iPad is out of reach or too much. Printable boards are below.

The device is never a consequence

We do not remove a child’s voice because of how they used it. Taking the device away for throwing, for pressing the same button fifty times, or for saying something rude teaches one lesson: that the voice can be taken. A child who learns that stops trusting the device, and stops using it. We would never tape a speaking child’s mouth for a bad word.
  • Repeating one button. Almost always exploration or self-regulation. Respond to it, model something new nearby, lower the volume if needed. Never remove the device.
  • Rude or silly words. Respond the way you would to speech, then move on. A child who used the device to make a social move just used language. That is progress.
  • Throwing or hitting with it. Keep everyone safe. If the device must move out of range for a moment, immediately offer the printed board so the voice is never gone. Use a protective case. Bring the device back when calm.

Proloquo2Go

By AssistiveWare. Most Casey students who use Proloquo2Go are on the Crescendo vocabulary: core words on the home screen, folders along the bottom, and button colors that follow parts of speech.

The colors have meaning

  • Orange: people and pronouns (I, you, he, they)
  • Pink: action words (want, go, help, eat)
  • Green: little position words (to, in, on, up)
  • Blue: describing words (good, more, all done)
  • Black tab: folders (Food, Places, Feelings)

How-to basics

  • Find a word. Most messages begin on the home screen. If you get lost, tap Home. Use the search (magnifying glass) to see the path to a word, then model it from that path.
  • Change a button. Tap the pencil in the bottom toolbar to enter edit mode. Tap the button. Change the label (what you see) or the message (what it says). Pick a symbol or a photo. Tap Done.
  • Add a new word. In edit mode, tap an empty space in the right folder, add a label, message, and picture. Real photos work well for family members, pets, and places.
  • Hide a word instead of deleting it. Hidden buttons keep their spot, so the layout stays the same and the motor plan stays intact. In edit mode they look faded. They can come back later.
  • Lock editing. Settings → Restrictions. Turn on the editing lock so changes happen on purpose.
  • Back up. Settings → Backups. Make a backup before any big change. The SLP keeps a copy.

TD Snap

By Tobii Dynavox. Our TD Snap students use the Core First page set: core words in the center grid, topic pages on the left, and quick-fire messages on the right. The core grid stays in the same place on every topic page, so “want more” has the same motor plan on the Food page and the Play page.

A few things to know

  • Grid size grows with the child. Core First comes in sizes from a few buttons to 60 or more. Moving up a size adds words without moving the ones already learned.
  • Hide and show buttons. Open Edit, tap a button, and toggle it off. The spot stays empty so nothing shifts. Bring it back later with one tap.
  • Quick fires. The one-tap messages on the right (Yes, No, Help, Break) are the fastest way to protest, ask for help, or ask for a break. Model them often.

Printing tips

  • Print in color on cardstock if you can, then laminate. A sheet protector works too.
  • Put one where the device is not: the car, the bath, the kitchen table, the playground fence.
  • Use it the same way as the device: point to the words as you talk. Wait. Respond to any point, look, or touch.
